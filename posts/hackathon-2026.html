HACKATHON 3 Days SEP 2026  ·  10 min read

Cybersecurity Hackathon 2026
3 Days, 11 Flags

A free, beginner-friendly workshop on web, mobile, and red team fundamentals — capped off with a CTF I didn't want to stop solving. This is the experience, not the writeups. Full technical breakdowns are linked at the bottom.

#hackathon #ctf #redteam #ignite #owasp
Duration
3 days, 9AM–5PM
Format
Training + Workshop CTF
Focus
Web, Mobile, Red Team
Challenges Solved
11
// GOING IN

Honestly, I didn't expect any of this to be free. A 3-day cybersecurity workshop, full training days, a live CTF at the end — I went in assuming it'd be a paid thing I'd have to justify to myself. It wasn't. And the content quality caught me off guard too — this wasn't a "beginner workshop" in the padded, watered-down sense. They genuinely tried to cover as much real ground as they could in three days: capturing and reading requests/responses, how testers actually approach mobile application security, current threat trends and perceptions in the industry. Beginner-friendly, but not beginner-shallow.

The trainers made the difference. The Blue Team trainer was solid — clearly knew the material. But the Red Teaming trainer was something else — chill, approachable, the kind of teacher who makes a technical room feel relaxed instead of intimidating. That mattered more than I expected.

// Context: I've been self-teaching cybersecurity for about 3 years at this point. Red teaming, offensive concepts, tooling — I had a decent handle on those. Web hacking specifically was the gap. I understood pieces of it but never had anyone actually clarify it end-to-end. That gap is exactly what this workshop closed.
// DAY ONE — NERVES
01Walking in nervous

First day, I was nervous — new environment, new people, and the organizer (a senior, Aurangzeb Rao) had actually asked me beforehand if I wanted to play in the Hackathon itself. I gave a hesitant "maybe... sure." Not exactly confident. First-day energy was cautious more than anything.

// DAY TWO & THREE — LOCKED IN
02Settling in

By day two, the nervousness was gone. The pace of the training and how genuinely interesting the content was meant I stopped thinking about the room and just focused on the material. Nine AM to five PM, every day, with a single 40–45 minute break — and I never once wanted to cut out early or waste a minute of it. That's rare for me to say about any training format.

03What actually clicked

The Red Teaming trainer specifically walked through request/response capture and manipulation in a way that finally connected the dots for me on web hacking — not just "here's Burp Suite," but why each step in an attack chain works the way it does. Three years of self-study had given me the offensive mindset already; what I was missing was someone clarifying the web-specific fundamentals properly. He gave me exactly that, and encouraged me directly to keep pushing in the field.

// BEYOND THE TRAINING

It wasn't just technical content either. The organizers talked about where this can actually go — Ignite sponsoring student teams and startups coming out of these programs, with prize pools in the range of ~12 million (PKR), plus OWASP and TryHackMe vouchers handed out. The message throughout was consistent: they want more people actually entering this field, not just attending a workshop and forgetting about it.

// THE WORKSHOP CTF

The three days closed out with a hands-on CTF spanning web exploitation, network forensics, cryptography, Android reverse engineering, and DFIR. I ended up solving 11 challenges — from session forgery and IDOR bugs on the web side, to reconstructing a flag out of a GRE covert channel, to tracing a full simulated APK-side-loading intrusion for a DFIR challenge.

I'm not re-explaining the technical details here on purpose — that's what the individual writeups are for. If you want the actual methodology for any of them, they're all documented properly:

→ Full technical writeups: github.com/zudio-infosec/ctf-writeups/hackathon-2026
// CLOSING THOUGHT

Going in nervous and coming out having solved 11 challenges and gotten a straight answer on where my web-hacking gaps actually were — that's a good three days by any measure. If a free workshop like this shows up again, sign up. Don't hesitate the way I almost did.
